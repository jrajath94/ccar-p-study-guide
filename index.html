  1. Skim the exam facts above so you know the weights and the question style.
  2. Study the domain volumes in order. Every unit ends with a "How they will ask this" box — read it twice.
  3. Work the question bank one domain at a time, then mixed. Aim to finish 63 items in under 120 minutes.
  4. Use the system design guide to build architectural judgment — that is what CCAR-P actually tests.
  5. Re-check model pricing and feature availability in the official docs before exam day; marked figures in the volumes are illustrative.

What actually helps (from candidates who passed)

The exam tests architectural judgment under constraints, not documentation recall. Every passer says the same thing in different words: it tests decisions, not features. Much of what it asks has no documentation page behind it, because it is asking how an architect behaves, not how Claude behaves.

  • Read the constraint first, then the architecture. When a scenario arrives, ask: is this primarily a cost problem? Latency? Reliability? Context? Safety? Multiple answers will look technically reasonable; only one fits the actual constraint.
  • Apply the simplicity test. What is the simplest approach that satisfies the stated constraints? More agents, more tools, and more abstraction are not automatically better.
  • Never default to the bigger model. Model selection is always a trade-off decision, not a size decision. Choosing the bigger model "to be safe" is one of the exam's favorite traps.
  • Name the trap, not just the concept. Give each mistake a name: Guard-Instead-of-Remove, Wrong-Layer Diagnosis, Blunt-Instrument Optimization, Vibes-Based Evaluation. A mistake you can name is one you can recognize among options you are seeing for the first time.
  • Draw the system on paper. Candidates who passed drew architecture diagrams by hand and mapped how parts depend on each other.
  • Explain your wrong answers out loud. When you miss a practice question, don't memorize the answer — break down why the correct approach works better than the one you picked.
  • Hands-on is not optional for Integration. Build a small MCP server, break a RAG pipeline's chunking on purpose, watch a cache hit-rate change. Judgment comes from touching the system.

The 2-week intensive plan (4+ hours a day)

Assumes 3+ years of architecture experience and some hands-on LLM exposure. Confirm your Partner Network eligibility on day 1. About 60 hours total.

Week 1 — build judgment (days 1–7).

  • Day 1 (4h): Read the official Exam Guide v1.0 end to end. Self-assessment: rate yourself 1–5 in each of the 7 domains. Start a mistake log with five tags: Concept (didn't know it), Boundary (knew it, used it in the wrong scenario), Reading (missed "first step" / "best" / "root cause"), Engineering (picked workable but unreliable), Terminology.
  • Day 2 (4h): Domain 1 (17%). Pattern selection: augmented call vs workflow vs agent. Drill: 10 scenario questions, identify the constraint before reading the options.
  • Day 3 (4h): Domain 2 (13%) + hands-on. Model selection trade-offs, prompt caching (static prefix first). Run a caching experiment and measure the difference.
  • Day 4 (4h): Domain 3 part 1 (19%). RAG: chunking, indexing, retrieval. Change chunk size and top-k on paper, predict what moves.
  • Day 5 (4h): Domain 3 part 2. MCP, tool design, least privilege, auth. Exercise: remove an unneeded tool from a design and justify why removal beats logging.
  • Day 6 (4h): Domain 4 (16%). Eval harness: metrics (accuracy / latency / cost / safety), a small eval dataset, A/B setup. Learn: evals as acceptance criteria agreed before the build.
  • Day 7 (4h): Timed mock 1. Score per domain. Tag every miss. For each miss, write why the correct approach works better — not just the answer.

Week 2 — calibrate and harden (days 8–14).

  • Day 8 (4h): Domain 5 (14%). Guardrail placement, human review routed by stakes, what GDPR / HIPAA / FedRAMP each constrain. Practice separating disqualifying constraints from merely expensive ones.
  • Day 9 (4h): Domain 6 (14%). Write a one-page architecture doc for a non-technical stakeholder. Practice the feasibility verdict — including "no".
  • Day 10 (4h): Domain 7 (7%) + weak-domain mop-up. Claude Code team setup, settings layers, debugging loop. Then attack your two lowest-scoring domains.
  • Day 11 (4h): Timed mock 2. For every wrong answer, write one sentence naming the trap.
  • Day 12 (4h): Scenario reading drills. 40 questions under a timer: read the last sentence first (the ask), find the constraint, eliminate "technically possible but wrong" options.
  • Day 13 (4h): Timed mock 3 — dress rehearsal, same time of day as the real exam. Full flag-and-review procedure.
  • Day 14 (2–3h, light): Re-read your one-page domain summaries and your named-trap list. Rest. No new material.

Exam-day tips

  1. Pace is 1:54 per item. 63 items, 120 minutes. Answer what you know on the first pass; flag long scenarios and multi-response items, then return.
  2. Read the last sentence first. That is usually the actual ask. Then read the stem for the constraint.
  3. Find the constraint before the options. Cost, latency, reliability, context, safety, output format. Say it in your head, then judge each option against it.
  4. Eliminate "technically possible but wrong." Kill over-engineered solutions, anything with high operational overhead, anything that ignores the stated constraint.
  5. When two answers both look reasonable, pick the simpler architecture that satisfies the requirements.
  6. Never choose the bigger model as the tiebreaker. The exam rewards the right-sized model, prompt caching, and context strategy.
  7. On multi-response items, read the selection count first. Each item tells you how many responses to select. Select exactly that many.
  8. 720 is a scaled score, not 72%. Anthropic doesn't publish the raw conversion. Don't do mental math mid-exam; just answer every question.
  9. Answer how Anthropic would. When torn, pick the answer matching the official docs' position, not the cleverest hack.
  10. Use the full time. No bonus for finishing early. Review flagged items, but only change an answer when you can name the reason.
  11. Tech-check the day before (online proctored): run Pearson's system test on the exact computer and network you'll use. Private room, one screen, webcam, mic, stable connection, no VPN.
  12. The retake wait is real (14/30/90 days between attempts). Treat it as single-shot — a reason to be careful, not to panic.
