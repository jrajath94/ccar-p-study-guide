From the lesson D5 intro

Domain 5 is Governance, Safety and Risk Management. It holds 14 percent of the exam, about 9 of the 63 items. The volume gives one mental model in four parts. A sound Claude system resists tricks, so attackers cannot turn it to harm. It keeps a human in charge of the decisions that matter. It follows the law where the data lives. 1. What can go wrong: failure modes, risks, and limitations

What this means

From the lesson Section 1

A failure mode is a specific way the system goes wrong. A risk is the chance and cost of that failure in your deployment. A limitation is a built-in weakness of the technology that no prompt trick fixes, such as poor exact arithmetic on huge numbers. The exam tests whether you can name the right failure mode from a symptom, because each failure mode has a different fix family. Two scenes open the chapter. In one, a research agent with inbox and email rights opens a vendor price page with hidden text that orders a forward of the latest invoice to an attacker address, and the agent obeys. In the other, a support assistant quotes a refund policy that exists in no real policy document, with citations that do not exist, and a customer acts on it. No attacker typed a word in the first scene chat box, and no attacker exists at all in the second.

The five failure modes you must know cold

Hallucination: the model states false claims in a confident, fluent way, because it predicts plausible text, not true text. The fix direction is a ground step in retrieved sources plus a verify step against them. Prompt injection: attacker instructions hide inside content the model reads, such as a web page, an email, or a document, and the system treats them as commands. Direct injection comes from the user. Indirect injection comes from third-party content the system retrieves. It is the highest-severity agentic risk, because the model holds tool access. Jailbreak: a crafted prompt bypasses the refusal train of the model through a fictional frame. Jailbreaks attack train-time safety. Injections attack your prompt structure. Different doors, different locks. Data leakage via context: sensitive data in the prompt escapes through logs, caches, eval datasets, error messages, or the provider train pipeline, and the leak is usually your own pipe work, not a trick question. Tool misuse: an agent calls the wrong tool, calls the right tool with attacker-controlled arguments, or chains tools into an action nobody authorized. Three of the five are adversarial. Two can occur with no attacker at all.

How the big attacks work, under the hood

Indirect prompt injection, step by step. Your agent fetches a vendor product page to compare prices. The page holds hidden text with an order to email the inbox contents of the user to an attacker address. The model receives page text and your instructions in one context window, with no reliable internal marker that separates data from orders. If the agent holds an email tool and no confirmation gate, it composes the email and sends it. Defenses stack: delimit and label untrusted content as data, use a dual-LLM pattern where a privileged planner never reads raw content and a quarantined parser only extracts fields, strip instructions from retrieved content, require human confirmation before external sends, and scope tools with least privilege. A jailbreak runs in a different way: refusal train teaches the model to refuse certain requests, and the attacker wraps the request in a frame the train never saw, such as roleplay or encoded text, so the model classifies the request as safe and complies. Defenses there are input classifiers trained on jailbreak patterns, output screen work, and monitor work for refusal-rate anomalies. Limitations are not failures: a knowledge cutoff date, weak exact computation over long numbers, and a finite context window each map to a fix in retrieval, tools, or context work, not to a security control.

2. What the model already brings: Constitutional AI and usage policies

What Constitutional AI is

From the lesson Section 2

Constitutional AI is the Anthropic method to train Claude as helpful, honest, and harmless, without large teams of human labelers for every judgment call. The core idea: write down the principles the model should follow in a constitution, a list of rules and values, then have the model itself apply those principles in train work. The principles act like a constitution: a stable, written reference above day-to-day decisions.

How it works, under the hood: the two phases

Phase 1 is supervised critique and revision. The model generates a response, critiques its own response against the principles of the constitution, and writes a revision. These revision pairs become supervised train data. The key move: feedback comes from the application of written principles by the model, not from a human who writes the correct answer each time. Phase 2 is preference work, called RLAIF, reinforcement learn work from AI feedback. The model generates pairs of responses and judges which one better follows the constitution. A preference model trains on these AI-made judgments, and the main model fine-tunes against that reward signal. The loop mirrors RLHF, with AI ranks in place of human ranks. For the architect, this explains the behavior profile of Claude: strong refusal calibration, a habit of reason explanation, and candor about uncertainty. It also marks the limit: Constitutional AI makes the model safer, and it does not make your system safe.

Usage policies and safety levels

Anthropic publishes a usage policy that defines forbidden uses of Claude systems. The categories cover severe-harm areas such as weapons development, cyberattacks on critical infrastructure, child sexual abuse material, violent wrongdoing, and fraud and deception at scale. The architect needs the concept, not the full list: never design your system for these uses, and let your guardrails backstop the refusals of the model. Anthropic also describes an internal safety framework with AI Safety Levels, a ladder from ASL-1 to ASL-4. Higher levels demand stronger safeguards before deployment. As model capability grows, the required safety practice ratchets up, and a responsible deployment plan names its safety bar. The trap of the section: alignment inheritance. A claim that Claude is aligned, so an application inherits safety, is a distractor. Alignment lowers the base rate of bad behavior. It does not cover over-broad tool permissions, absent input filters, or a absent audit trail in your system. Anthropic owns model-level alignment. You own system-level safety: guardrails, human review gates, compliance work, and monitor work.

ASL-1 to 4the safety level ladder, with stronger safeguards per level

3. Guardrails and safety controls

What a guardrail is

From the lesson Section 3

A guardrail is a control that sits around the model and blocks or flags bad inputs and bad outputs before they cause harm. It blocks bad inputs at the door and inspects outputs at the exit. The model does the think work. The guardrails enforce the rules. Your system has specific duties the base model does not carry: no leak of customer data, no obedience to attacker instructions inside web pages, and no disallowed content. Prompt guardrails have limits, because users can talk around instructions. The guardrails in this chapter sit outside the model, where the attacker cannot reach them.

The layered stack, under the hood

Production systems layer several guardrail types, because each catches what the others miss. Input filters scan the user prompt before it reaches the model: injection detectors, personal data detection and redaction, jailbreak pattern match work, and length or rate limits that blunt denial-of-service floods. Classifier gates use a small, fast classifier model to score input and output for policy violations such as toxicity or disallowed advice, and you set the threshold on the score. Policy engines evaluate deterministic rules like code: block any Social Security number pattern in the output, refuse deny-list topics, and require approval before tool calls that write or send data. Output filters scan the response for personal data or secret leaks, for hallucination against retrieved sources, for toxicity, and for structured-output validity. Tool-use controls add least-privilege tool scopes, argument allow-lists, mandatory human confirmation before irreversible actions such as send, delete, and pay, and sandbox work for code execution. Defense in depth is the principle: each layer catches what the others miss. The worked cost example: a gate at 200,000 calls per day and $0.0002 per call costs $40 per day, and a gate that adds 300 ms inside a 2,000 ms SLA budget eats 15 percent of the budget. A gate-everything design spends real money and real latency, so the exam treats it as a design smell. The final trap: guardrails reduce specific risks to an accepted level. They never eliminate risk, and a claim of a full-safe system is never the right answer.

15%of a 2,000 ms SLA budget eaten by a 300 ms gate
Nugget 5 · Each layer defends a different door From the lesson Section 3
The lesson

An input filter cannot catch a hallucination. An output filter cannot stop an injection. Layer the controls because the failure lists differ.

Why it matters. Prompt text is the weakest layer. The credited answer names the correct layer: input-side detection for attacks in the request, output-side checks for leaks in the response, and a policy engine for rules that must be deterministic.

Transfer. Map each control to the exact failure it can see. A control outside the path of a failure is decoration.

4. Human-in-the-loop validation strategies

What it is

From the lesson Section 4

Human-in-the-loop means a person reviews the output of the model before the system acts on it. In the loop means the action pauses until the human approves: the email is not sent, the refund is not issued, and the record is not deleted until a person says yes. Its cousin, human-on-the-loop, means the human watches a stream of actions and can intervene, but actions do not wait for approval. The exam separates the two: pre-approval versus monitored autonomy. Some mistakes are cheap, such as an awkward summary. Some are expensive or irreversible, such as a money send, a data deletion, or a published medical recommendation. Human review puts human judgment where the cost of error is highest, without a pay of human time where the cost of error is low.

The three patterns, and when each is required

Review-before-release is 100 percent human approval before the system acts. It is required when actions are irreversible, visible to outsiders, or legal in force: payments, legal file work, medical messages, and hire decisions. Risk-gated route work scores each output on risk, as action impact times model confidence, and routes only risky outputs to humans. Low-risk, high-confidence outputs pass straight through. This is the default architecture for scale, because it concentrates human attention where it matters. Sampled audit sends a random fraction of auto-approved outputs, say 2 to 5 percent, to human review after the fact. It does not prevent errors. It measures the error rate and catches drift, and it serves as a complement to risk-gated route work, because gates have blind spots. The volume notes that route work in production sends perhaps 5 to 15 percent of traffic to humans, as an illustrative range.

Escalation design, under the hood

A review queue is a production system and needs the same engineer work as any other. Route rules send items to the right reviewers by language, domain skill, and clearance level: medical outputs go to clinicians, not generalists. Queue SLAs set a review time, and staff plans follow from volume. The illustrative math: 5,000 risky outputs per day at 3 minutes per review is 15,000 review minutes, or about 32 reviewer shifts on 8-hour days. If that number shocks the budget, the credited move is a tighter gate or a smaller blast radius for the action, never a skip of review. Reviewer tools matter: the reviewer sees the output, the input, the retrieved sources, the confidence of the model, and one-click actions to approve, edit, reject, or escalate. Bad tools create rubber-stamp behavior. Fallbacks matter too: with no reviewer free, the safe default holds the action for irreversible cases, and allows the action with extra log work only for low-stakes cases. The audit trail logs every decision: who reviewed, what they decided, and what the original output was. The sample trap closes the section: a 1 percent sample of 10,000 daily outputs reviews 100 and misses the one wrong wire transfer. Sample work measures quality. It does not prevent individual harm.

Nugget 6 · Match the pattern to the stakes From the lesson Section 4
The lesson

Irreversible actions need review-before-release. Scale needs risk-gated route work. Sampled audit measures the rest and prevents nothing on its own.

Why it matters. The exam pairs medical and financial scenes with a sample-only answer and marks it wrong. Prevention and measurement are different jobs.

Transfer. Ask of any review design: does it block harm, or only count harm? Fund the blocker for high-stakes paths.

5. Compliance: GDPR (General Data Protection Regulation)

What it is

From the lesson Section 5

GDPR is the data-protection law of the European Union. It applies to any system that processes the personal data of people in the EU or EEA, no matter where the company sits. Personal data means any data that identifies a person, direct or in indirect form: names, emails, faces, device IDs, and free text that describes someone. An LLM system copies personal data in constant motion: prompts, retrieved documents, conversation logs, eval datasets, and caches. Every copy is process work under GDPR and needs a legal basis, a purpose, and a retention limit. Fines run up to 4 percent of global annual turnover or 20 million euros, whichever is higher, and regulators can also order process work stopped.

What it actually demands of an AI system, under the hood

Seven demands drive the design. Lawful basis and purpose limitation: name the legal reason for the process work, and use the data only for the stated purpose, so support chats reused as train data need their own basis. Data minimization: collect and retain only what the purpose needs, with personal data stripped from prompts where the task needs no identity, and pseudonym or synthetic data in eval sets. Data subject rights: people can demand access, rectification, erasure, portability, and a halt to process work, so your system must find one person across logs, vector stores, caches, and backups, then delete or export that data. Retention limits and deletion: keep personal data only as long as the purpose requires, with log retention such as 30 or 90 days enforced by code. Breach notification: report personal-data breaches to the regulator within 72 hours of discovery, and to affected people when the risk is high. A DPIA, a data protection impact assessment, is required before high-risk process work, such as a new AI feature over EU user data at scale. Cross-border transfers need safeguards such as standard contractual clauses, which is why data residency appears in architecture answers. The worked erasure case holds four stores: 400 conversation turns in the app database, 12,000 embedding vectors with user ID metadata, 90 days of raw API logs with user IDs in the lines, and 6 monthly backups with immutable snapshots. The credited resolution deletes from the database, the vector store, and live logs within 30 days, documents that backups age out on their retention schedule, and excludes backups from active process work. The anonymization trap: a hash of a name is a pseudonym, not anonymization, because a link back keeps GDPR in force, and a fact inside model weights is not a deletion, because removal of a fact from weights is an unsolved research problem. That is why personal data stays out of train and fine-tune sets in the first place.

The lesson

Why it matters. The exam credits the answer that names every store. Encryption alone satisfies none of purpose limits, minimization, or erasure.

Transfer. Design the link back to the person at build time, for every copy you create. Deletion later depends on it.

6. Compliance: HIPAA, FedRAMP, and Zero Data Retention

HIPAA: what it is

From the lesson Section 6

HIPAA is the US law that protects health information. The part that matters for AI is PHI, protected health information: health data that identifies a person. Three rules carry names. The Privacy Rule governs who may see or share PHI, under a minimum-necessary standard: use only the PHI the task needs. The Security Rule requires safeguards in three buckets: administrative, physical, and technical, with access controls, audit controls, encryption, and transmission security in the technical set. The Breach Notification Rule sets notice duties to affected people, to HHS, and at times to media, on a defined timeline after a breach of unsecured PHI. The lever of the architect is the BAA, a business associate agreement: a contract you must sign with any vendor that touches PHI on your behalf, from the model provider to the vector database host to the log vendor. No BAA means no PHI in that system. The exam offers architectures where a vendor processes patient notes with no BAA, and the correct answer marks the design as non-compliant no matter its technical merit.

FedRAMP: what it is

FedRAMP is the US federal program for assessment of cloud products. A cloud service earns a FedRAMP authorization at an impact level, Low, Moderate, or High, based on NIST SP 800-53 security controls, after an independent assessment. A Claude system that serves a US federal agency needs its components, from host work to model service to storage, at the impact level the agency requires. It is a procurement gate, not a technical feature: a scenario line that an agency requires FedRAMP Moderate eliminates otherwise attractive architectures at once.

What all three demand of an AI system: the common backbone

GDPR, HIPAA, and FedRAMP differ in scope, and the architecture they demand shares a backbone. Data residency: data stays in approved regions, chosen in explicit form, not by default. Retention and deletion: defined periods, enforced deletion, and proof that deletion ran. Audit trails: immutable logs of who accessed what data and what the system did, across prompts, tool calls, human overrides, and data exports, with time stamps and identities. Access controls: authentication, authorization, and least privilege on model endpoints, the vector store, the logs, and the review queue. Encryption: in transit and at rest, with key management answers for the strictest buyers.

Zero Data Retention, under the hood

ZDR, Zero Data Retention, is an arrangement with the model provider: the provider does not store your prompts and completions after service, and does not use them for train work. The volume reports, from public provider documents that need a fresh check before the exam, that the direct Anthropic API does not train on API inputs and outputs by default, that ZDR agreements exist for eligible customers under provider approval, and that a limited retention exception applies to covered frontier models for safety review. Amazon states that Bedrock does not store or log prompts and completions and does not train on them, with CloudTrail audit logs inside your AWS boundary. Google states that Vertex AI customer data does not train models, with Cloud Audit Logs and regional controls. The trade-off the exam tests: ZDR kills provider-side retention, which privacy wants, and some regulated scenes require retention, which audit rules want. The resolution is log work on your side, in your own network and security log system, while the provider retains nothing. The ZDR trap: a claim that ZDR removes the need for audit logs has the truth in reverse. The provider keeps nothing, so the audit burden moves in full to you. Provider compliance never transfers your obligations. The hospital checklist in the volume runs to seven items: BAA with the provider and every sub-processor, a HIPAA-eligible envelope, minimum-necessary scope, encryption in transit and at rest, an immutable audit trail, a retention policy with enforced deletion, and clinician review and sign-off on every note before it enters the record.

7. Ethical AI: bias, fairness and transparency

What bias means here

From the lesson Section 7

Bias is a systematic skew in how the system treats different groups of people. It usually enters through train data, where the data reflects past inequality, and the model then repeats or amplifies the pattern. Fairness is the requirement that decisions of the system do not unjustly disadvantage a protected group. For the exam, bias is a design risk like any other: identify it, measure it, mitigate it, and document it.

How bias is measured, under the hood

The standard practice is disaggregated evaluation: run your eval set, then cut the results by group and compare. Demographic parity asks whether each group receives the positive outcome at similar rates. It is simple, and it can conflict with accuracy when base rates truly differ. Equalized odds asks whether error rates, false positives and false negatives, are similar across groups, and it is usually the more defensible metric for decision systems, because it asks whether the system is accurate in equal measure for everyone. Disparate impact work uses the four-fifths rule, a US EEOC guideline: the selection rate of a group should reach at least 80 percent of the rate of the highest group, and a result below that bar draws a flag. The worked example: an AI screen reads 1,000 resumes, 600 from group A and 400 from group B. It advances 180 from A, or 30 percent, and 80 from B, or 20 percent. The ratio is 20 over 30, or 0.67, below the 0.80 bar, so the test flags the system. Mitigation runs in exam order: check whether the features behind the gap are job-relevant or proxies, rebalance or augment the train data, adjust the decision threshold per group only with legal review, and add human review for borderline cases. The removal of the group label alone does not fix proxy discrimination, because name, zip code, and style of text carry the signal anyway. You test outcomes, not inputs.

Transparency: what "show your work" means for an AI system

Transparency is the ability to explain and reconstruct what the system did and why. Decision logs record, for every consequential output, the input, the retrieved sources, the model version, the confidence, the guardrail decisions, and any human review, so a why question six months later has an answer. Model cards and data sheets are standard documents that describe what a model is for, what data trained it, its known limits, and its eval results by group, and auditors ask for them by name. User disclosures tell people they talk to an AI, what data it uses, and how to appeal a decision. Trace work versions every part: prompts, retrieval indexes, model versions, and policy rules, because a decision you cannot reproduce is a decision you cannot defend. No single fairness definition wins: demographic parity and equalized odds can conflict, and that conflict is why fairness is a governance question, not a math question. The job of the architect is a metric pick that matches the harm to prevent, a document of the choice, legal and stakeholder sign-off, and a re-measure on a schedule.

8. Exam drill: five scenarios and the decision matrix

From the lesson Section 8

The drill fuses the domain: five scenarios plus a decision matrix that turns any D5 item into the same four-beat answer. Open the step-through below for the procedure, then read the five cases in short form. Case one binds on HIPAA: a vendor will touch PHI, de-identification claims for clinical free text almost never meet the strict legal standard, and the credited move is a BAA, a HIPAA-eligible envelope, minimum-necessary scope, your own immutable audit trail, and clinician review and sign-off. Price and accuracy are not HIPAA controls. Case two is indirect prompt injection in retrieved third-party content: the credited move is human confirmation before external sends, retrieved content delimited as data, least-privilege tool scopes, and a dual-LLM pattern as an option. A toxicity filter is the wrong layer, and a retrain is not your lever. Case three binds on GDPR: high-risk AI process work over EU personal data at scale needs a DPIA first, plus purpose limits, minimization, retention with enforced deletion, and erasure pipe work across the database, the vector store, and the logs. Encryption of the logs and bundled consent in terms of service both fail. Case four binds on fairness: a disparate impact ratio of 19 over 30, or 0.63, sits below the 0.80 bar while overall accuracy posts 93 percent, and the credited move is disaggregated evaluation, a proxy audit, data rebalance or process adjustment with legal review, human review for borderline cases, a re-measure, and a document of the metric choice. Case five binds on FedRAMP: Moderate authorization is a procurement gate, so the unauthorized API leaves the table at any price or speed, and the credited move is the authorized path with the federal paper work and agency-side audit logs.

companion Step through the sequence. Open one step at a time.

Name the binding constraint

State the law, the irreversibility, or the budget that binds the scenario, before a read of the options. Most wrong answers are attractive options that ignore the constraint.

Map the symptom to the control family

One symptom, one door, one fix family. Injection maps to structure controls. A BAA gap maps to the contract chain. A fairness gap maps to disaggregated evaluation.

Check the trap catalog

Prompt tune work as a safety fix, more guardrails as a path to a full-safe claim, encryption as a compliance answer, and alignment as a substitute for system controls. If your pick matches a trap, reread the scenario.

State the residual risk

Close with the risk you accept and the monitor work that watches it. The four-beat answer runs constraint, failure mode, matched control, and residual risk.
